Interior Painting in Palm Beach County: Prep Steps That Matter

By Art Lego LLC · South Florida · October 29, 2026

Good prep decides how interior paint looks when the job is done. In Palm Beach County, the steps that matter most are patching, sanding, priming, and masking. Skip one of them and the finish will show it.

Why Prep Matters More Than the Paint

Most homeowners spend time choosing a color. That choice matters, but the surface under the paint matters more. Paint does not hide holes, dull spots, or old glossy patches. It copies them. A flat wall with a poor base will look uneven, even with the best paint on top.

Good prep takes time. It is also the part of the job you cannot see once the room is finished. That is why it often gets rushed. At Art Lego LLC, we treat prep as the foundation of every interior job in Wellington, West Palm Beach, and the rest of our service area.

Step 1: Patch the Walls

Start by looking at the walls in raking light. Hold a flashlight low and walk along the wall. Dents, nail holes, cracks, and old anchor holes will show up as shadows. Mark each one with painter's tape or a pencil.

Fill small holes with a lightweight spackle. Use joint compound for larger cracks and dents. Press the filler in firmly, then let it dry completely. Most fillers shrink a little as they dry, so a second thin coat often gives a smoother result.

Once the filler is dry, sand it flush with the wall. The patch should feel like part of the wall, not a bump under your hand.

Take a photo of each wall before you start. Photos help you spot flaws you missed, and they make it easy to review the work with your painter later.

Step 2: Sand for Smoothness and Grip

Sanding does two jobs. It smooths rough spots and roughens glossy surfaces so new paint can grip. A light pass with fine-grit paper is usually enough on drywall. Trim, doors, and cabinets often need more work because their finish is harder.

Wipe every surface after sanding. Dust left on the wall can cause small bumps and weak spots in the paint. A damp cloth works well. Let the surface dry before you move to the next step.

Sanding also tells you where the wall needs more work. If a patch still shows after sanding, fill it again and repeat the steps. Two thin layers of filler work better than one thick layer that cracks.

Step 3: Clean Greasy and Stained Areas

Kitchens, hallways, and the areas around light switches collect grease, dirt, and fingerprints. Paint does not stick well to these spots. Wash them with a mild cleaner, rinse, and let them dry. Stains from water, smoke, or marker need to be sealed before you paint, or they may bleed through the new color.

Step 4: Prime Before You Paint

Primer is not optional when you patch a wall, cover a stain, or switch from a dark color to a light one. Primer seals the surface and helps the topcoat go on evenly. It also keeps patched areas from looking dull, which is when the sheen changes from one spot to another.

Use a primer made for the job. A stain-blocking primer works best on marks from water or smoke. A bonding primer helps when you paint over slick surfaces. Ask the store staff which one fits your project, or ask us during your estimate.

Step 5: Mask Carefully

Good masking gives you clean lines at the ceiling, trim, and corners. Use painter's tape made for interior work. Press the edge down firmly so paint does not creep underneath. Remove outlet covers and switch plates instead of taping around them. It takes a few minutes and gives a cleaner result.

Cover floors and furniture with drop cloths. Plastic sheets can slip on hard floors, so use canvas or thick paper where you can.

Plan your order of work before you open the first can. Start with ceilings, then walls, then trim. Drips and dust fall downward, so this order keeps finished areas safe. Note which walls get the most sun and which spots see the most traffic. Those areas often show wear and flaws first, so give them extra attention during prep.

Why the Finish Depends on Prep

Paint shows every step that came before it. Gloss reflects every bump. Flat paint turns uneven patches into dull spots. Dark colors reveal thin coverage, and light colors reveal stains. When the prep is done well, the final coat looks even, and the room feels finished.

South Florida adds its own challenges. Humidity, sun, and salt air all affect how paint cures. For more on how our local climate changes the process, read our guide on interior painting in Florida humidity.

A Simple Prep Checklist

  • Remove furniture, wall hangings, and outlet covers.
  • Inspect walls in raking light and mark every flaw.
  • Patch, let dry, and sand smooth.
  • Wipe away dust and clean greasy spots.
  • Prime patches, stains, and color changes.
  • Mask trim, ceilings, and fixtures.

Frequently Asked Questions

How long does interior prep take?

Prep time depends on how many walls need patching and how much sanding is needed. Most rooms need a full day of prep before any paint goes on, and some older homes need more.

Do I need primer for every paint job?

Not always, but primer is important when you patch a wall, cover a stain, or switch from a dark color to a light one. It helps the topcoat look even.

Why does paint show every flaw on the wall?

Paint copies the surface under it. Bumps, holes, and dull patches show through, especially with glossy paint and in raking light.

Can I skip sanding and just paint over the wall?

You can, but the result may look uneven and the paint may not grip well on glossy or rough areas. A light sanding usually makes a visible difference.
