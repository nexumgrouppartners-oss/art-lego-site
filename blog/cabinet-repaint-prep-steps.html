Repainting Kitchen Cabinets Without Peeling: Prep Steps

By Art Lego LLC · South Florida · November 9, 2026

Cabinets peel when grease and shine are left on the surface. To avoid it, clean the wood well, sand it, and prime it before any finish coat goes on. Skipping one of those steps is the usual reason a new coat of paint lifts. Art Lego LLC handles this work through its cabinet and trim painting service.

Art Lego paints kitchen cabinets, bathroom vanities, baseboards and trim work with durable finishes. The steps below show how a careful job goes, so you know what to expect and what to ask about before anyone opens a paint can.

Why do painted cabinets peel?

Paint peels when it cannot grip the surface. On cabinets, the cause is almost always cooking grease, hand oils or a glossy factory finish that was never roughed up.

Kitchen cabinets take the worst of it. Steam, splatter and daily handling leave a thin film that you can barely see. Paint applied over that film sits on top of it instead of bonding to the wood. In a humid place like Wellington, moisture in the air makes a weak bond fail even sooner.

Doors and drawer fronts near the stove and sink peel first. Edges and corners that get touched the most follow. If you see peeling in those spots, the prep was likely rushed.

What gets cleaned and sanded first?

Every surface gets degreased first, then sanded. Cleaning removes the film you cannot see, and sanding gives the primer something to hold.

Here is the order a good prep follows:

  1. Remove the doors, drawer fronts and hardware, and label each one so it goes back where it came from.
  2. Degrease every door, frame and drawer front with a cleaner made for kitchen grime.
  3. Rinse off the cleaner and let the wood dry fully.
  4. Fill dents, old hardware holes and chips, then sand the filler smooth.
  5. Sand all surfaces to dull the old shine, including edges and the profile of each panel.
  6. Wipe away the dust so it does not get trapped under the primer.
  7. Apply primer, then the finish coats, letting each coat dry before the next.

Dust is the step people forget. Sanding dust left on a door shows up as rough spots in the finished coat. A clean wipe before priming makes a visible difference.

Why does priming matter on cabinet surfaces?

Primer is the layer that locks the paint to the cabinet. Without it, the finish coat rests on a slick or stained surface and can scratch off.

Primer also blocks stains. Older wood can bleed tannins, and kitchen surfaces can hold grease stains that sanding does not fully remove. A good primer seals them so they do not show through light colors.

On painted or sealed cabinets, a bonding primer helps new paint stick to a hard, smooth surface. Ask your painter which primer they use and why it suits your cabinets. A clear answer is a good sign that the prep is planned and not improvised.

Which parts are painted: doors, frames, drawer fronts?

A full cabinet repaint covers the doors, the face frames and the drawer fronts. The painter should tell you up front if any part is left out.

The face frame is the visible box around the doors. If it stays old while the doors turn new, the two will not match. Cabinet sides that you can see, such as an end panel by the dining area, also need paint so the whole run looks like one piece.

How do you know the finish is ready for use?

A cabinet finish is ready when it feels dry and hard, not just dry to the touch. Paint can feel dry on the surface while still soft underneath.

Drying and curing are two different things. Dry means the paint no longer feels wet. Cured means the film has hardened enough to handle daily use. Humid South Florida air can slow that process, so a coat that looks finished may still need time.

Ask your painter how long to wait before closing doors tightly, putting dishes back, or wiping surfaces. Gentle handling at first protects the finish while it hardens. Rushing it is a common way to leave fingerprints and dents in a new coat.

What should you ask a painter before starting?

Ask how they prepare the surface, what primer and paint they use, and how they handle doors and hardware. A painter who has a clear plan will answer easily.

  • Will the doors and drawer fronts come off for painting?
  • How is grease removed before sanding?
  • Which primer goes on, and why?
  • How many finish coats are planned?
  • How long before the cabinets can be used normally?

These questions also tell you whether the estimate covers real prep or just a quick coat. If the answers are vague, the prep may be thin.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can you paint over glossy cabinets?

Yes, but the shine has to be sanded down first and a bonding primer applied. Paint put straight over gloss has little to grip, and it usually scratches or peels early.

Do cabinet doors need to be taken off to paint?

Removing doors and drawer fronts gives a cleaner result. It lets the painter sand and coat every edge and keeps paint out of hinges and hardware. Labeling each piece keeps everything in the right spot.

Why did my new cabinet paint turn sticky?

Sticky paint is often a sign it has not cured yet, or that grease was left under it. Humidity can slow curing. Give it more time and handle it gently before judging the finish.

Is it worth repainting old kitchen cabinets?

If the boxes are solid and the layout works, repainting can refresh a kitchen without replacing everything. A painter can check the wood and tell you whether the surfaces will hold new paint.
