Cabinet and Trim Painting: Prep That Makes It Last

By Art Lego LLC · South Florida · November 5, 2026

Paint on cabinets and trim usually fails because of poor prep, not bad paint. Most chipping, peeling, and sticky doors come from skipped steps. Here is what a careful crew does before the first coat goes on in Wellington and nearby cities.

Cabinets and trim take more abuse than most walls. Kitchen grease, hand oils, and daily bumps wear down a finish fast. Trim around doors and windows gets scuffed by shoes, pets, and furniture. Paint needs a clean, dull, and dry base to stick well, so prep matters most on these surfaces.

Decide Whether the Doors Come Off

The first big choice is whether to take cabinet doors and drawer fronts off the frame. For most kitchens, removing them is the better choice. A flat door lies level on a table, so paint goes on evenly with no drips in the corners. It also keeps overspray off the countertops and appliances.

Removing doors takes more time at the start. Label each door and its hinge location with painter's tape before you unscrew anything. Number the drawer fronts too. This small step saves a lot of frustration when it is time to put everything back.

Trim is different. Baseboards, door casings, and window frames stay on the wall. Tape the wall edge carefully and cover the floor with a drop cloth. Work in small sections so the paint does not dry before you finish a clean edge.

Clean Off the Grease First

Cooking grease is the biggest enemy of paint adhesion. Even a thin film of oil can cause paint to peel within months. Wipe every surface with a degreasing cleaner made for kitchens. Use a clean cloth and change it often, because dirty water just spreads the grease around.

Pay extra attention to the areas above the stove, beside the sink, and near cabinet pulls. Hands touch these spots every day, so they carry more oil than the rest of the cabinet. Rinse with clean water and let everything dry fully before you move on. Skipping the rinse leaves a residue that can ruin the next step.

Sand for Grip, Not for Bare Wood

Sanding gives new paint something to hold onto. The goal is to dull the old finish, not to strip it down to bare wood. Use a fine sanding sponge or 180 to 220 grit sandpaper. Sand with the grain on wood, then wipe away the dust with a damp cloth.

Glossy finishes need more sanding than satin ones. Sanding also fills tiny scratches and gives the surface an even look. If the old paint is already peeling, scrape the loose spots first. Then sand the edges smooth so they do not show through the new coat.

Use a Bonding Primer

Primer is not optional on cabinets and trim. It seals the surface, blocks stains, and helps the topcoat bond. A bonding primer made for slick surfaces works best on glossy or previously painted wood. A stain-blocking primer is a good choice when old water stains or wood tannins might bleed through.

Apply primer in thin, even coats. Brush or roll with the grain on trim, and smooth out any drips while they are still wet. Let the primer dry for the full time on the label before you sand it lightly. A smooth primer coat makes the finish coat look far better.

Watch the Humidity and Temperature

South Florida weather affects paint more than most people expect. High humidity slows drying and can leave a soft, tacky finish. Very hot, direct sunlight can make paint dry too fast, which leaves brush marks and lap lines. Indoor work in a closed room often has the same problem if the air conditioning is off.

Paint in a room with steady temperature and moving air. Run a fan, but point it away from wet paint so dust does not blow onto the surface. Check the label for the best temperature range and follow it. When you can, plan the work for a dry day with mild temperatures.

Give the Paint Time to Cure

Drying and curing are not the same thing. Paint may feel dry to the touch in a few hours, but it is still soft inside. Curing takes much longer and happens as the paint hardens. Heavy use too early can scratch the finish, so give it time before you close the doors.

Wait for the manufacturer's recommended time before you rehang doors. Cabinet doors need extra care, because the front and back of a door can stick together if they touch too soon. Place doors back on the frame gently and avoid slamming them. Keep dishes and heavy items off the shelves for the first few days.

Pick the Right Topcoat

Choose a durable topcoat made for kitchen and bath use. Cabinet paints often come in satin, semi-gloss, or enamel forms. Satin hides small flaws and cleans easily. Semi-gloss shows more wear but handles heavy scrubbing well. Trim usually looks good in a sheen that matches the room.

Apply thin coats and let each one dry before you add another. Two thin coats look better than one thick coat. Thick paint can drip and take much longer to cure. A fine foam roller or a quality brush gives smooth lines on the doors.

When to Call a Professional

Some prep work is simple enough for a careful homeowner. Other parts need experience. Cabinet painting takes patience, a clean workspace, and good tools. If you have many doors, complicated trim, or limited time, a professional crew can save you days of work. A pro also knows which primers and sheens hold up in our climate.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do I need to remove cabinet doors before painting?

For the best finish, yes. A flat door is easier to paint evenly, and it keeps paint off the frame and countertops. Label every door and drawer so it goes back in the right place.

Can I paint cabinets without sanding?

Light sanding is needed. You do not need to strip the finish, but the new primer and paint need something to grip. Skipping this step is a common reason paint peels.

How long should painted cabinets cure before use?

Follow the manufacturer's recommended time on the label. Paint can feel dry much sooner, but it stays soft while it cures. Avoid heavy use and slamming doors during that time.

Does humidity affect cabinet paint in South Florida?

Yes. High humidity slows drying and can leave a tacky finish. Plan painting for a drier day with steady temperatures when you can.
