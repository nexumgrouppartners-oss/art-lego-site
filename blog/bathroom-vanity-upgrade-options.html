Bathroom Vanity Upgrade Options in Boynton Beach

By Art Lego LLC · South Florida · November 10, 2026

Swap the vanity if the layout stays and the rest of the bathroom works. Plan for more work if the plumbing moves. Art Lego LLC lists new vanities alongside waterproofed showers, tile and frameless glass for Boynton Beach bathrooms, so a vanity swap can be a small job or part of a larger remodeling project.

This guide helps you sort out which one you have. It covers the signs that a vanity swap is enough, the choices that hold up in a humid room, and what changes when pipes have to move.

Is a new vanity the right upgrade?

A new vanity is the right upgrade when the walls, floor, shower and layout still work and the cabinet is the part that bothers you. If the room works and the vanity is the problem, a swap is a sensible, contained project.

Look at the signs first. A vanity is a good place to start when you see any of these:

  • The doors or drawers are swollen, peeling or hard to close.
  • The cabinet base is soft near the floor or around the sink pipes.
  • You have no room for towels, cleaning supplies and daily items.
  • The top is stained, chipped or cracked around the sink.
  • The style clashes with the rest of the room, but the room itself is fine.

A soft base or a stain under the sink can mean a slow leak. Look at that before you buy anything. A new cabinet set over a hidden leak will fail the same way the old one did.

If the tile, the shower and the floor are also tired, a vanity alone may leave the room looking uneven. In that case, think about the whole bathroom. Which vanity choices suit a humid bathroom?

In South Florida, pick a vanity built to handle steam and splashes, with a solid frame, sealed edges and a top that does not soak up water. Humidity is the main test for any bathroom cabinet here.

Here is what to compare:

  • Size. Measure the wall space and the swing of the door. A wider vanity gives more storage but can crowd the toilet or the entry.
  • Storage. Drawers hold small items better than a single open door. Check where the drain pipe sits, because it can cut into drawer space.
  • Materials. Ask how the cabinet box is made and how the edges are sealed. Raw edges near the floor soak up moisture over time.
  • Top and sink. A one-piece top with the sink built in has fewer seams to leak. A separate top and sink look different but need good sealing.
  • Floating or floor-standing. A floating vanity opens up the floor and is easy to clean under. A floor-standing one often holds more.

Think about how your family uses the room too. A shared bathroom needs room for more than one person's items. A guest bath can use a smaller, simpler cabinet.

What changes when the plumbing moves?

When the sink and drain stay where they are, a vanity swap is a simple job. When you want the vanity in a different spot or a different size, the plumbing has to move, and the project grows.

Moving plumbing can mean opening the wall or the floor. That brings in patching, new tile or flooring in places, and more coordination between trades. It is the point where many homeowners decide to look at the whole bathroom instead of one cabinet.

Matching the old plumbing location keeps the work smaller. If you are changing from a single sink to a double, or from a pedestal sink to a cabinet, ask about it early. Those changes often need more than a simple swap.

Does the vanity affect the paint and finish?

Yes. A new vanity changes the wall behind it, the baseboard beside it and the line where the top meets the wall. Plan for paint and trim touch-ups when the cabinet goes in.

Old cabinets often hide a patch of unpainted wall or a different paint color. When the cabinet comes out, you see it. Painting after the vanity is in keeps the lines clean and covers any marks from the install.

Choose a paint finish that handles moisture. Bathrooms get steam every day, so a washable, moisture-friendly finish is a better pick than a flat one. Our painting service can handle that step as part of the job, so you do not need a second contractor for the walls.

Also think about the mirror, the light above it and the outlets nearby. If you swap to a wider or narrower vanity, those pieces may sit in the wrong spot. It is cheaper in time and trouble to decide this before the cabinet arrives.

What should the walk-through cover?

The walk-through should cover the plumbing location, the wall and floor condition, the vanity size and the finish work. Bring your questions and measurements, and expect Art Lego to look at the whole room, not only the cabinet.

Use this list to prepare:

  1. Take a photo of the current vanity and the wall behind it.
  2. Measure the width, depth and the space around the door and the toilet.
  3. Note any soft spots, stains or odors near the base.
  4. Decide whether the sink and drain can stay where they are.
  5. Pick a rough style and the storage you need.
  6. Ask what the estimate includes for paint, trim and cleanup.

Ask who handles each step and how the work will be sequenced. A clear plan for removal, install, paint and cleanup keeps a small project from dragging on. When is a vanity swap not enough?

A vanity swap is not enough when the shower leaks, the tile is failing or the layout does not fit your daily routine. In those cases, the vanity is only one symptom.

If the shower needs work, new vanities can wait until the larger work is planned, so the finishes match. Doing the cabinet first and tearing out the floor later can mean damage to the new piece. Talk through the order of work with your contractor.

If you only want a fresh look and the rest of the room is sound, a vanity swap with new paint can make a clear difference without a full remodel.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I keep my old sink and just change the cabinet?

Sometimes. It depends on the sink style and how the top is made. Many tops and sinks come as one unit, so changing one often means changing both. Ask during the walk-through so the plan matches your room.

How do I know my vanity is leaking?

Check for stains, soft wood, mildew smells or warped panels at the base of the cabinet. Empty the cabinet, look at the pipes with a flashlight and run the water. Fix any leak before you install a new vanity.

Should I replace the vanity before or after painting?

Usually install the vanity first, then paint the walls and trim. That way the paint covers marks from the install and the lines stay clean. Your contractor can set the order of work when they plan the job.

Does a bigger vanity always mean more storage?

Not always. The drain pipe and the layout of drawers decide how much space you can actually use. A well-planned drawer set in a smaller cabinet can beat a larger cabinet with one open door.
